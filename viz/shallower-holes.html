Switching your 401(k) won't beat the market. It can make the holes shallower.

I tested five simple once-a-month rules, including a hidden Markov model, on 30 years of S&P 500 and bond index fund data, with every signal computed only from data available at the time. None reliably earned more than holding. The best two cut the worst loss roughly in half.

Why that 100% backtest means less than it looks

The screenshot that started this counted 830 of 830 "completed sequences" and then blacked out the time each one took. A rule that waits indefinitely for a price to come back will almost always finish in a market that rose for three decades. The useful questions are how long the wait was and how far it fell first.

Here is the same kind of test on the Nasdaq-100 since 1987: every time it dropped 10% from a high, did it get back to that high?

Each dot is one 10%-or-worse decline, placed on a log scale by trading days until the old high was regained. Bigger, darker dots fell further.

It completed 33 of 33 times, a perfect 100%. The median recovery took 44 trading days. One took 3,921 trading days, from April 2000 to November 2015, after falling 83%. A hit rate tells you nothing until it comes with the worst wait and the worst depth.

The differences in yearly growth are not statistically meaningful; every switching rule's edge over buy-and-hold has a t-statistic below 1 in absolute value. What is consistent is that the trend and momentum rules cut the worst loss from 55% to 29–34% and shortened the longest wait from six years to under two and a half.

What the hidden Markov model saw

A two-state hidden Markov model assumes the market is always in one of two unseen moods, each with its own typical return and volatility, and estimates the odds of being in each from recent weekly returns. Here it was refit every January using only prior data, and the monthly signal used the forward-filtered probability, never the smoothed one that peeks at the future.

The model found a calm state averaging about +18% a year at 12% volatility and a stressed state averaging roughly −20% at 28% in recent fits. The rule moves to bonds when the stressed state is more likely than not.

Top: growth of $1 in the S&P 500 fund (log scale). Bottom: the model's month-end probability of the stressed state.

It caught 2008, 2020 and 2022 well, and in the 2010s it came closest of the switching rules to matching buy-and-hold. But the 2000 to 2002 bear market was a slow grind broken up by sharp rallies, and the model kept flipping in and out, so it still lost 47% and spent almost seven years below its high. A volatility detector is a crash detector, not a bear-market detector.

Trend rules earned their keep in the two big bear markets and paid for it in the 2010s, when choppy dips triggered exits that missed the rebound. That trade-off is the price of the shallower holes, and it can last a decade.

Is 10 months special?

No, which is good news. If only one lookback worked, it would be a sign of curve-fitting. Every moving-average length from 6 to 16 months gave similar growth and a worst drop between 20% and 34%.

Doing it in a 401(k)

  1. Pick two funds from your plan's menu: the S&P 500 or total-market index fund, and the bond index or stable value fund.
  2. On the last trading day of each month, check whether the stock fund's price is above its average of the last 10 month-end prices. A free monthly chart with a 10-period moving average shows this.
  3. If it's above, hold the stock fund. If it's below, move to the bond or stable value fund. Do nothing in between.
  4. Before the first switch, read your plan's frequent-trading rule. Many block re-entry for 30 to 90 days after a round trip. These rules switched between once every two years and about twice a year, which most plans allow.
  5. If going all-in or all-out feels too blunt, apply the rule to half the balance and leave the other half invested. Over this period that version grew at a year with a worst drop of .

Switches inside a 401(k) don't trigger taxes, which is the main reason these rules suit it better than a taxable account.

Method. Daily total-return prices for Vanguard 500 Index (VFINX) and Total Bond Market Index (VBMFX), with the 13-week T-bill rate as cash. The plan was to use Quantiacs futures data, but its futures endpoint returned server errors throughout, so public fund data stood in; the fund series are the closer match to a 401(k) menu anyway. Signals use month-end closes and trade at the next close. No fees, redemption charges or plan restrictions are modelled. The HMM is a two-state Gaussian model on weekly log returns, refit each January on all prior data from 1987, five random starts, filtered probabilities only. Sharpe ratios are measured against the T-bill rate.

This is research, not financial advice. Past drawdowns don't bound future ones, and none of these rules is guaranteed to beat holding the fund.
